— TRADING PSYCHOLOGY —

Thinking in Probabilities

Scenario: You just lost 3 trades in a row.

Your emotional brain says: "Something is wrong with my strategy." Or: "The next trade will probably lose too."

The probability-based trader says: "Those three trades are finished. The next trade is a new probability."

The market doesn't remember your previous trade. If your setup has a genuine edge, you don't need to know "Will THIS trade win?" The question becomes "Did I execute my setup correctly?" That's the whole psychological shift.

The 5 Fundamental Truths

60% edge, 12 wins / 8 losses overall — but the order is random. The same streak that opened this page shows up right in the middle, and the system is still profitable.

1Anything can happen — even your best setup can lose.
2You don't need to know what happens next to make money — you only need an edge over a series of trades.
3There is a random distribution between wins and losses — your next 5 trades could be W L L L W or L L L L L, even if your strategy is profitable.
4An edge is simply a higher probability — not certainty. A 60% setup can still lose.
5Every moment in the market is unique — the previous trade does not determine the next one.

The Dog Analogy

Same mechanism as Trade #1 and Trade #2 losing — your brain flags Trade #3 as dangerous even though it's a completely independent setup.

Imagine a child seeing a dog for the first time. He's curious and excited — he has no previous experience with dogs. Unfortunately, the first dog he meets attacks and bites him. After that, whenever he sees another dog — even a completely friendly one — his mind automatically connects it with the painful memory. He becomes afraid, because his brain has learned: Dog = danger. But the second dog isn't necessarily dangerous — his previous experience is distorting how he reads the new situation.
Trade #1 → Loss. Trade #2 → Loss. Then your setup appears again — Trade #3, the exact same valid setup. But now your brain says: "Careful! This looks like the trades that lost." You hesitate, reduce your position, move your entry, or skip the trade entirely. Now flip it: Trade #1 → Win, Trade #2 → Win, the exact same setup appears — and the opposite bias (overconfidence) kicks in instead.

The Key Lesson

The next trade is not the previous trade. A valid setup has its own probability distribution. Your last loss doesn't make the next trade more likely to lose, and your last win doesn't make the next trade more likely to win.

Dog #1 bit me ≠ every dog will bite me.
Trade #1 lost ≠ Trade #2 will lose.

That's one of the central ideas behind thinking in probabilities — trading without becoming emotionally attached to the outcome of any single trade.

— MANAGING DAMAGING EMOTIONS —

Success in trading is more about emotional control than technical mastery. These emotions will try their best to take over you during every trade — the goal isn't to never feel them, it's to acknowledge each one so you keep control over your actions.

1. FOMO

Buying after the move already happened — right where the risk/reward has flipped against you.

Trading without being objective — the itch to trade because you see the price going up. You may not have a trade plan before buying, or a mabilisang trade plan. Makikita mo lang ang ibang options and things to consider AFTER executing the trade: like resistance na pala, or maliit pala ang RRR, and worst is wala namang valid na entry point nung bumili ka.

Always keep in mind: there will always be another play coming. You have to ACCEPT that you missed a trade, and that you will trade with greater risk if you chase the price. Prices move in cycles — if you miss the move of a stock, it may create another setup, and there will be many other stocks that make the same move, whether the same swing or the same breakout. Maiwan ka man ng NOW, DITO, MAC, MWIDE — madami pang dadating na plays. Same setup, same plays, iba-iba lang ang stock code.

So simple lang ang FOMO: acknowledge it, ACCEPT na naiwan ka. When you know this, you will not chase the price.

MANTRA"I am a disciplined trader. I will not trade without a Trade Plan."

2. Fear of Loss

Exiting on the first wobble, not on your plan — the trade was fine, your nerves weren't.

Common after a series of losses. Natakot na ang trader, nawalan na ng confidence. One sign: kapag nag-move up na ang price, kahit 4% pa lang, binenta na — sayang ang trade plan. Hindi nabigyan ng enough space ang price action na gumalaw. Remember: hindi laging pataas ang price, it moves in waves to reach a certain level.

To rebuild confidence: mentally prepare yourself first. Purge and take your mind off trading by doing other things to keep you busy. When you're calm and ready again, start trading virtually or with a small allocation — 5–10% of the portfolio. REMOVE the need to be right all the time. Hindi lahat ng losses ay bad trades, same with winning trades — following your TRADE PLAN is what makes the trade a success. Confidence builds as you gain more data and experience. Also, lean on your support group whenever these emotions hit — you're not the only one who's gone through this.

Building/rebuilding confidence: a newbie trader on a setup needs "familiarization" — backtest first, to see how the setup forms and ends. Then forward-test with a small allocation, to practice your execution. Smaller allocation = less emotion. Once you know how to execute the setup cleanly, that's when you move up to your normal allocation.

3. Greed

Adding bigger tranches the closer price gets to resistance — sizing up on hope, not on a plan.

Tranche-buying into resistance just because you see the price going up. Or oversizing your first entry. FOMO and overtrading are also forms of greed — they all come from a need to win, a desire for gain, and the thought of winning clouding your judgment.

CONTROLJust like FOMO and every other emotion here: FOLLOW YOUR TRADE PLAN. Pre-planning is what gets you through the chaos and uncertainty where hasty decisions get made.

4. Pride / Ego / Superman Syndrome

A streak builds confidence — overconfidence sizes the next trade as if it can't lose.

Hindi pababaan ang AEP, or palakihan ng gains — lalo na within the group. Walang yabangan dito. We only trade our objectives and our trade plans. As long as you REMAIN OBJECTIVE, you followed your trade plan, and you LEARN from it — big or small the gain — then it's a GOOD TRADE.

When trading, we are against OURSELVES. Our emotions — how we control them — will dictate how we control our trades. We trade what we see on the chart and react to the price action; no one can predict the exact bottom to buy or the exact top to sell.

Laging tandaan: hindi mo objective na makuha ang 100% of the rally. Also, a winning streak doesn't mean your next trades will also win — this is where the superman syndrome / overconfidence / ego problem comes from. After a big gain, consider stepping back or withdrawing gains to reset your portfolio, or keep going as long as you stay objective. This is also where DENIAL (not accepting the trade didn't go your way), FEAR OF BEING WRONG, and HOPE (that price comes back to lower your loss) come from.

Dapat ba nating masalo ang bottom? Dapat ba tayong sa peak nakakabenta? Our objective is to execute well. Profit is just the by-product.

5. Regret

Both paths were unknown when you sold — only one is real, and you followed the plan either way.

"I should have held. I should have sold. I should have bought."

Trading is about PROBABILITIES — we build our trade plans around them. If your plan was to sell and you sold, that's a good trade. If price goes up right after you sell, some traders feel regret anyway. But what if you hadn't sold, hadn't followed your plan, and price had dropped instead? Whatever happens next — after selling, after trading your plan, or watching a rally you didn't plan to buy — the objective is always to EXECUTE YOUR PLAN.

We build our trade plans before the market opens, WHEN WE ARE CALM AND OBJECTIVE — when we can see the probabilities clearly. We must accept we can be wrong, and price may keep going after we sell, or rebound after we cut. RESPECT YOUR TRADE PLAN — it was built with risk management and other safety protocols in place. Avoid intraday adjustments to the plan, where hasty and impulsive decisions get made; that skill comes later, but for now is the time for learning and practice. We only react to the price triggers we already defined. Hesitating to follow the plan — like not cutting when your cut-loss is hit — only turns into a bigger loss.

Acceptance

1Accept that you cannot be 100% right all the time.
2Accept that you cannot catch the peak and bottom. Always practice an "Attitude of Gratitude."
3Accept that you will lose in trading — which is why we have risk management. Our goal is to make money AND keep the money we made.
4Accept that you will miss some trades. There will always be another play.
5Accept that there is no perfect system. Every system has its pros and cons.
— HOW TO READ THE CHART —

A step-by-step way to zoom from the big picture down to your actual entry — three passes, each one narrowing the picture further. Do them in order, every time, before placing a trade.

Step 1 — Set Your Bias with Fibonacci Retracement

On the Weekly or Daily timeframe, plot a Fibonacci retracement from swing high to swing low (or the reverse, for a downtrend). A simple tool, but this is what sets your overall bias before anything else.

DISCRETIONARY Which swing you plot from is a judgment call, not a fixed rule — it depends on which horizon you're actually trading:
Longer-term (Position/TF): the whole major swing — the highest high to the lowest low of the broader structure (often spanning months). Sets bias for trades you intend to hold for weeks.

Medium-term (Swing): the most recent significant swing — the latest major high to the latest major low within that bigger structure, not all the way back to the start of it.

Shorter-term (Scalping): the most recent local swing only — the latest minor high to minor low. Still read on Weekly/Daily per Step 1, but zoomed to whatever leg is actually relevant to a trade you'll hold for hours, not weeks.

Whichever horizon you pick, stay consistent with it for the rest of the read — Step 2's boxes and Step 3's entries should zoom in from the same swing you anchored your bias to, not a different one.

Price comes down off a downtrend and consolidates below the 23.6% level. Between 11.8% and 23.6% reads as Position-trade territory; the deeper below 11.8%, the closer it is to the swing low itself.

Price pushes up and closes through the 38.2% level — this shifts the bias from Position over to an Emerging Trend, tradeable as Swing.

Price clears the 50% level and holds above it — still Swing, but with smaller remaining upside now that the real target is the 61.8% golden-ratio level.

61.8% is the golden ratio — price gets rejected here most of the time. Only once it closes above AND consolidates above 61.8% does this confirm continuation — the common setup behind MYH / ATH plays, with 88.6% marking the retest of the old swing high itself before new highs.

Step 2 — Plot Boxes to Find the TSL Zone

Next, break the big picture down to the medium picture: plot your Darvas-style boxes and locate the Trend Shift Line (TSL) — the same TSL/Box concept already used in Stage 4 of the Checklist tab. This narrows your Step 1 bias down to the actual zone price is trading in right now.

Recap: TSL HIGH is the highest Box_Top among the prior boxes to the left — the resistance that, once closed above, confirms a new trend and invalidates the old range. TSL LOW is the Box_Bottom of the current/most recent box — a close below that shifts the trend back down. Price sitting "Near TSL" means it's testing one of these two lines, not yet broken through.
TRADER RULEDon't jump from the Fib bias straight to an entry — the TSL zone is what tells you WHERE inside that bias price actually is right now.

Step 3 — Drop to 1H and 5-Minute for Entries & Execution

Once your bias (Step 1) and your zone (Step 2) are both set, drop down to the 1H and 5-minute charts purely for timing — the actual trigger, entry price, and execution. These lower timeframes answer "when do I click buy/sell," not "should I be bullish or bearish."

TRADER RULEThe lower timeframe never overrides the bigger picture. If the 1H/5m setup looks good but Steps 1-2 disagree, that's a lower-probability trade — not a reason to flip your bias.

Elliott Wave Theory: Complete Guide

Principles, rules, every wave's personality, and a full breakdown of A‑B‑C corrections.

Core principles

The full cycle 5 with trend3 against

Motive waves (with trend) Corrective waves (against trend) Rule boundary

The 6 big ideas

  1. 5‑3 pattern: markets advance in 5 waves with the trend and correct in 3 against it.
  2. Motive vs corrective: waves 1, 3, 5 (and A, C) are motive. Waves 2, 4 (and B) are corrective.
  3. Fractal: each wave is made of smaller waves. A wave 1 contains its own 1‑2‑3‑4‑5.
  4. Degree matters: a 15m "wave 5" can be a tiny part of a daily wave 1.
  5. Fibonacci proportions: waves relate by 0.382, 0.5, 0.618, 1.0, 1.618, 2.618.
  6. Crowd psychology: each wave has a typical mood (doubt, fear, conviction, boredom, euphoria).

The 3 unbreakable rules break one = wrong count

  1. 1
    Wave 2 never retraces more than 100% of wave 1 It can't go below wave 1's start. This is your invalidation for a wave 3 trade.
  2. 2
    Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5 It doesn't have to be the longest, just never the shortest.
  3. 3
    Wave 4 never enters wave 1's price territory Exception: diagonals (wedges), where overlap is normal.

Rules for corrections too

  • Zigzag: wave B never goes beyond the start of wave A.
  • Flat: wave B must retrace at least 90% of A. Less than that means it isn't a flat.
  • Triangle: each leg is a 3-wave move, and in a contracting triangle each leg is smaller than the one before.
  • Combination: at most one triangle, and only as the final part.
  • A triangle never appears as wave 2, and wave 2 is never a combination ending in a triangle.
If price breaks a rule level, don't argue with the market. Recount immediately, because that level is your stop.

How to count wave 1

Four steps

  1. The prior trend looks complete: a finished 5-wave move or A‑B‑C, at support, with RSI divergence.
  2. The rise is impulsive: 5 sub-waves on a lower timeframe. A 3-wave rise is usually just a correction.
  3. It breaks structure: a close above the last lower high (change of character).
  4. Wave 2 confirms it: the pullback holds above wave 1's start, then price breaks wave 1's high.
You don't trade wave 1, you find it. The trade is wave 3, entered during or after wave 2.

Common wave 1 mistakes

  • Labeling every bounce as wave 1 without checking for 5 sub-waves.
  • Calling a 0.236 pullback a finished wave 2. It's usually only the first dip.
  • Placing the stop under wave 2's low instead of wave 1's start. The true invalidation is wave 1's start.
  • Ignoring the higher timeframe. A clean 15m wave 1 inside a daily downtrend is often a B wave.

Trading the correction

  • Zigzag: after B, trade C toward C = A. Stop beyond B.
  • Expanded flat: fade B's breakout only with confirmation. The C target is 1.618× A.
  • Triangle: don't trade inside it. Trade the break after e, or the retest of the broken line.
  • The best trade is usually the end of C: it's the start of the next impulse in the bigger trend's direction.

Diagonals (wedges) the only place overlap is allowed

Ending diagonal reversal

  • Appears in wave 5 or wave C, at the end of a trend
  • 3‑3‑3‑3‑3 structure. Wave 4 overlaps wave 1.
  • Both lines slope the same way and converge
  • Often a throw-over (fake break) of the line at wave 5
  • The reversal usually returns quickly to the wedge's start

Leading diagonal

This is the rarer cousin: it appears in wave 1 or wave A and is followed by a deep wave 2 (often 61.8–78.6%). It signals a new trend, not the end of one.

Guidelines not rules, but they're right most of the time

Channeling

  • Draw the line from 2 to 4, then a parallel through 3. Wave 5 often ends near the upper line.
  • A throw-over above the channel at wave 5 is an exhaustion sign.
  • A break of the 2–4 line after wave 5 is early confirmation the impulse is over.

Other key guidelines

  • Extension: one of waves 1, 3 or 5 usually extends. In stocks it's mostly wave 3; in crypto and commodities wave 5 often extends.
  • Equality: when wave 3 extends, waves 1 and 5 tend to be equal in length.
  • Depth of correction: a correction usually ends in the territory of the previous wave 4 of one lesser degree.
  • Truncation: wave 5 failing to exceed wave 3 signals severe weakness, and a sharp reversal follows.
  • Volume: it should expand with wave 3 and contract during 2 and 4. Weak volume on 5 warns of the end.
  • Momentum: RSI peaks in wave 3. Divergence on 5 or C hints at completion.
  • Time: corrections often take 1–2× the time of the wave they correct. Sideways ones take longer.
